The piece at the top of the home page is not a video. It is two fragment shaders running in your
browser right now, and most of what looks like damage is the renderer showing you its own insides.

## Pass A: the sculpture

The form is a **gyroid**, a triply periodic minimal surface, carved into a sphere. A gyroid has
a compact implicit approximation:

$$
g(\mathbf{p}) = \sin p_x \cos p_y + \sin p_y \cos p_z + \sin p_z \cos p_x .
$$

Taking $|g|/k$ at frequency $k$ and subtracting a thickness gives a thin shell. Intersecting that
with a sphere (a `max` of two distance functions) gives the lattice. The result is not an exact
distance field, so the ray marcher takes cautious steps of $0.8\,d$ rather than $d$.

```glsl
float map(vec3 p){
  float k = 4.3 + .8*sin(T*.31);          // the lattice slowly breathes
  vec3 q = p*k;
  float g = abs(dot(sin(q), cos(q.zxy)))/k - .04;
  float body = max(length(p) - 1.12, g*.75);
  float core = length(p) - .42;           // a hot core inside the cage
  return min(body, core);
}
```

Shading is reflection-only: the reflected ray samples a procedural studio of two strip softboxes
and two coloured kicker lights. A thin-film term tints the reflections by view angle, which is
where the oil-slick colours come from.

## The second form: a superformula urchin

Every twenty-odd seconds, a burst carries the lattice into a spiked shell. Its outline comes from
the **superformula**, Johan Gielis's 2003 generalisation of the superellipse:

$$
r(\varphi) = \left( \left|\cos\tfrac{m\varphi}{4}\right|^{n_2} + \left|\sin\tfrac{m\varphi}{4}\right|^{n_3} \right)^{-1/n_1}.
$$

With $m = 19$, $n_1 = 9$, $n_2 = 14$, $n_3 = 11$ it draws a sunburst: nineteen identical spikes,
one every $2\pi/m$ radians.

The usual 3D version is a *spherical product* of two such curves, one around an axis and one
from pole to pole. That keeps the symmetry in only one direction, so the result looks like a
softly extruded star, not a sea urchin. To keep the 2D curve's symmetry in **every** direction,
this piece revolves each spike around its own axis instead:

1. Spike directions are spread over the sphere at the curve's own angular spacing
   $\Delta = 2\pi/m$: latitude rings $\Delta$ apart, each carrying about
   $2\pi\cos(\text{lat})/\Delta$ spikes, alternate rings staggered.
2. For a point $\mathbf{p}$, find the nearest spike direction $\mathbf{c}$. Only three rings can
   contain it, so this is constant-time. Let $\psi = \angle(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{c})$.
3. The surface radius in that direction is the 2D curve read outward from a spike tip,
   $R = r(\varphi_{\text{tip}} + \psi)$, clamped at half a spacing, where the valley between
   spikes sits.

So every spike has exactly the 2D curve's cross-section, whichever way you look. The gap
$|\mathbf{p}| - R$ is only a bound on the true distance (the spikes are steep), so the marcher
takes shorter steps while an urchin is on screen, and rays that graze a spike without converging
count as misses rather than speckle.

The morph between the gyroid and the urchin is a plain linear blend of the two distance fields.
Halfway through, the lattice appears to sprout spikes. Changes of species (sunburst, urchin,
star, pollen) happen at the peak of a glitch, where the jump in $m$ hides inside the corruption.

## The leak

During a burst, the frame is cut into macroblocks. Some blocks stop showing the beauty render and
show a debug buffer instead: **surface normals**, **depth**, or the **number of march steps** each
pixel took. These are the images a graphics programmer stares at all day. Here they become the
texture of the glitch.

## Pass B: corruption

The second pass reads the first as a texture and damages it the way a failing video decoder
would:

- **Slice tearing**: horizontal bands of random height shift sideways.
- **Macroblock displacement**: blocks sample from the wrong place, and some get posterized to four levels, like a datamoshed codec.
- **Pixel sorting**: in some rows, each pixel takes the brightest value from a short run to its left, so highlights drag into streaks.
- **Chroma split**: red and blue sample at offsets that grow with the burst and with distance from the centre.

Then ACES tone mapping, scanlines, a slow roll bar and a vignette. At the peak of a big hit, the
whole frame inverts for a single frame.

## Timing is the art

Random glitching is just noise. What makes it read as a *signal* is rhythm: long quiet passages,
single-frame flickers, then a hit. The glitch seed is also held for two frames, so blocks linger
long enough to register as errors rather than static. Scroll quickly or move the pointer and you
add energy to the next burst.

> Everything interesting in a renderer happens in the buffers you never ship.
